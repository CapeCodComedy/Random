A Husserlian Critique of Pannenberg’s Understanding of Subjectivity Kyung Phil Kim | ORCID: 0000-0002-9262-9142 Calvin Theological Seminary, Grand Rapids, MI, USA phil0717@gmail.com Abstract I argue that Wolfhart Pannenberg’s view of human subjectivity presupposes a metaphysics of eternity that both contracts and expands the human subject. For this purpose, Edmund Husserl’s phenomenology is a useful criterion. Pannenberg rejects substantialist theories of subjectivity that give priority to the agency of the ego over the passivity of the self. Following Friedrich Schleiermacher, Pannenberg thinks selfreflection must be grounded on a symbiotic totality of life, and he views essences, even of subjectivity, as determined by that totality. In contrast, Husserl provides both an essentialist theory of subjectivity and a viable model for self-reflection that does not suffer from infinite regress. Husserl does more justice to the mineness of consciousness and the agency of the ego, while not discarding the narrativity of the self. In this paper, I argue that Pannenberg’s view of human subjectivity presupposes a metaphysics of eternity that both contracts and expands the human subject. Second, I believe Husserl’s phenomenology to be the current best philosophical theory to explain human self-consciousness by viewing it as self-luminous. Human consciousness is by its nature self-aware. Thus, the question of how the ego posits the self does not face the problem of infinite regress. If Kant’s critique aimed to explain how natural sciences are possible, Dilthey’s is “a critique of the capacity of man to know himself and the society and history which he has produced,” or a critique that explains how human sciences (Geisteswissenschaften) are possible (Dilthey 1989, 165). Difference in research objects should lead to difference in methodologies. Natural sciences explain natural phenomena according to causality. However, “in the historical world there is no natural science causality . . . ; history only knows of relationships of agency and suffering, action and reaction” (Dilthey 2002, 219). For human beings have wills, and this volitional aspect gives a creativity that cannot be explained by natural sciences. The objects of human sciences are spiritual realities. First, there are “structural units,” which are “lived experiences” (Erlebnisse). Each of the units is a part of the whole. Lived experiences form a whole in one person’s life. Also, the psychic nexuses of individuals form a larger nexus that includes all the life courses of all individuals. Thus, for the human sciences, the world is not the entirety of the physical universe. “The completion of all relations contained in what is experienced and intuited would be the concept of the world” (63). Therefore, human sciences aim to understand the world as spiritual or to form an “epistemic nexus,” an “objectively valid conceptual cognition of the interconnectedness of lived experiences in the humanhistorical-social world” (23). Another difference between human and natural sciences is that spiritual realities have a “psychic structure.” A psychic structure is “the pattern according to which psychic facts of various characters are regularly connected with one another by an inner, experienceable relation” (35). Although natural sciences, too, start with data given in experience, the causality in natural phenomena cannot be experienced; it can only be inferred. When I see an apple falling from a tree, I do not see gravity itself. Gravity can only be inferred. Nexuses of natural phenomena also show rigidity and regularity. An apple falls down; it does not go up. On the other hand, psychic data are interrelated by an inner relation. Imagine that I am a physics student who is preparing for his first exam. When I sit down at my desk to begin studying, through the window I see an apple fall from a tree. This perception triggers a memory, and I start thinking about how I failed an important physics test in high school. Now, two things can happen. The memory may raise within me such doubts about my ability to pass the exam that I decide to not even study for the exam, as I feel that it is useless. Or the memory could make me all the more determined that this time is going to be different and inspire me to work extra hard in order to pass the exam. In my lived experience, a succession of thoughts might have occurred by some causality, but causality cannot predict whether I will choose to study hard for my physics exam or just give up. My lived experiences are in an inner relation that is not determined by causality. Further, this relation between the lived experiences is experienced. I feel regret, and I decide to study (or not). Dilthey further describes the inner relations as the relation of part and whole: the parts and whole of psychic nexuses refer to each other (36). For example, seeing the apple fall from a tree motivates me to study physics, at t1. As a result, I win a Nobel Physics Prize at t2. However, at t3, I build nuclear weapons that initiate a worldwide nuclear war. At t2, my experience of seeing an apple fall at t1 is what made me win a Nobel Prize (positive), but at t3, the t1 experience made me the cause of a nuclear war (negative). Why did Dilthey need a new critique of historical reason that is different from Kant and other metaphysics? Dilthey’s critique of the failure of human sciences of the past is described in his principle of phenomenality, which is expressed as follows: “Everything is a fact of consciousness, and accordingly is subject to the conditions of consciousness” (Dilthey 1989, 247). That is, the beginning point (principium) of the human sciences is the “fact of consciousness” (246). Objects, whether external or psychical, can be said to exist only if they are given in consciousness. If an object is not given or cannot be given in consciousness, then it is nonexistent. At first glance, this resembles Husserl’s claim that all psychic states of affairs are intentional. However, Dilthey’s application of this principle is much bolder. It claims that all sciences—even including mathematics—are experiential. By the principle of phenomenality, Dilthey rejects any kind of knowledge that claims to be atemporal or that is not conditioned by the psychic nexuses (1989, 247). One of the concepts considered false by Dilthey is the atemporal essence. If an essence were to be atemporal, an essence would not be an object of consciousness, which is by its nature temporally conditioned. For Dilthey, an essence is not a “thought entity” (ens rationis) that is independent of reality nor something that is hidden behind the sensory world (1989, 182, 236). Rather, an essence is a procedure that forms a conceptual nexus and thus is subject to constant change (2002, 263, 298). Since there are no fixed essences or fixed ideas, there is no objective validity that does not change. Objective validity, too, is something that constantly develops (2002, 160). The rejection of atemporal essences leads to the rejection of metaphysics. According to Dilthey (1989, 175), metaphysics is a subordination of what is real to the law of knowledge. Although metaphysics claims it is eternal and above time, it, too, is merely a human product (1989, 229). All knowledge, including metaphysics, is personally and historically conditioned. Philosophy is nothing other than “raising world-views to universally valid knowledge” (2002, 190). The historical multiplicity of metaphysics only shows the “relativity” and even “anarchy” of metaphysics (1989, 191–192). Only by the fall of metaphysics can a true critique of historical reason arise. The rejection of atemporal essences and metaphysics has an impact on the study of the human subject: there are no a priori forms of the human subject, understanding of subjectivity Philosophia Reformata 87 (2022) 49–70 This content downloaded from 75.68.252.89 on Fri, 10 Jul 2026 18:43:17 UTC All use subject to https://about.jstor.org/terms 54 nor is an a priori deduction possible (Dilthey 1989, 233, 235). The concept of substance is merely a heuristic concept (1989, 232). For Dilthey, Kant’s doctrine of the transcendental ego is nothing but an illusion of an isolated, self-sufficient intellect (264–265); Husserl also falls into the error of using abstract entities to explain the subject (Dilthey 2002, 257). The human subject is constantly in the flux of time, and therefore, it cannot be defined (Dilthey 1989, 246), nor can its essence be grasped (2002, 216). Humanity is an “indeterminate type” that develops in every psychic state (2002, 181). Rather than trying to search for an unmoving core of the subject, human sciences aim to understand the totality of lived experiences (Dilthey 2002, 298). In this vein, autobiography is considered “the highest and most instructive form of the understanding of life” (221). In other words, the true identity of a human being does not lie in its minimal core ego but rather in the sociohistorically conditioned self, in its story or narrative. Understanding the totality of someone’s life story requires the totality of all human sciences. For instance, to fully understand the life of Otto von Bismarck, one could use psychological insights to analyze his character; one could research the Prussian aristocracy to find roots of his habit of dominating; one could resort to politics to consider how various political groups put pressure on Bismarck; and so on (164). The meaning of one’s life constantly goes through a hermeneutic circle. Only by one’s death can the relationship between the parts and whole within one’s life course be finalized, and still this would not mean that the meaning of one’s life is finally determined. That could only happen at the end of history (Dilthey 2002, 253). Although we cannot grasp the totality of life nexuses, as human science develops, our horizon of understanding widens (2002, 299). Or, in Michael Ermarth’s words: “The ‘wholly of whollies’ we will never know, but the lesser coherences we can know with certainty” (Ermarth 1987, 79). In sum, Dilthey strictly holds to a principle of phenomenality and denies any abstract ideas that are detached from the stream of experience. This leads to a temporalized essentialism, which denies any metaphysics of the ego. 2.2 Husserl’s Understanding of the Transcendental Ego and Essence One of Dilthey’s sharpest critics was Edmund Husserl, whose understanding of the human subject and objective validity makes a clear contrast with Dilthey’s. Husserl argues that Dilthey’s historicism is a denial of rigorous science. On Dilthey’s view, says Husserl, there can be no philosophy that accounts for a supratemporal structure of the ego. However, he was too quick to view them as lacking objective validity, whereas strict sciences (or natural sciences) were deemed to have it (Husserl 1965, 124–125). Husserl argues that historical sciences cannot judge the existence of a valid philosophy but can only make historical judgments— they can only judge how a certain event in time is related to another event in time. Inner relations among time events in a time-lapse do not and cannot form or influence validities or essences. Thus, Dilthey’s error was a categorical one. Historical categories do not apply to validities and essences. For instance, mathematics does not implement the principles of historical studies; it is a study of supraexperiential essences. To derive necessity from experience is to try to drink water from a rock (ex pumice aquam) (Husserl 1965, 126–127). To claim that all metaphysics are wrong seems contradictory, according to Husserl. To judge that a philosophy is wrong implies that it can be fixed, he says. The ideal that something must be true remains intact regardless of a philosophy’s success. Thus, the denial of objective validity to any philosophy whatsoever seems to lead to a denial of validity itself, which defies Dilthey’s claim as well. By misapplying the principle of phenomenality, Dilthey falls into a “superstition of facts,” which is to “misinterpret ideas as facts and to transform all reality, all life, into an incomprehensible, idealess confusion of ‘facts’” (Husserl 1965, 141–142). Contrary to Dilthey, Husserl (1965, 129) argues that phenomenology is a “theory of essence,” for “it is the phenomenological theory of essence alone that is capable of providing a foundation for a philosophy of the spirit.” For him, essences are “what is to be found in the very own being of an individuum as the What of an individuum.” Husserl argues that essences are objects that are sui generis, namely, different from objects of sensual perception. Essences have their own way of intuition. In the perception of an object, say, a book, I can think of what a book is. The essence, the whatness of a book can be “put into an idea.” This process is called an “ideation” (Husserl 1982, § 3, 8–11). The intuition of an essence does not necessarily rely on actual experience. Although it can occur in sensual perception, it can also occur by mere phantasy (Husserl 1982, § 4, 10). For instance, when a geometer draws a figure on a board, his drawing does not “ground” his “geometrical seeing of essences and eidetic thinking” (§ 7, 16). Despite all the discrepancies between an ideal straight line and a drawn wiggly line, the geometer intuits the essence of a straight line. This shows that essences, as ideas, are independent of external objects and experiences of them. Here, the transcendental phenomenological ego could not have been grasped if intuition of essences were impossible and if the essential structures of consciousness or intentionality of the ego did not exist. In sum, although Husserl would agree with Dilthey that the human subject is bound to a temporal stream of consciousness, he disagrees that even ideas or essences are bound to temporality. Thus, contra Dilthey, he argues for a phenomenology that investigates the essential structure of the human subject. 2.3 Pannenberg’s Understanding of Essence and Self As mentioned above, Pannenberg sees essences as conditioned by temporality. An essence can only be determined by eternity, which is the totality of its duration in time. For Pannenberg, a change of essence means a change of meaning within different historical contexts. For example, he tries to solve the problem of transubstantiation in the Eucharist by the concept of transignification. “Transignification involves a change of meaning that does not alter the identity [i.e., substance] of a thing” (Pannenberg 1998, 300). At the Eucharist, the substance of the bread remains the same, while the meaning of it turns into “the body of Christ,” which is a change in essence. “The result is that we no longer view substance as an abiding basis for all changes in the sense of the personal identity that logically precedes all relation to others, for the relations in which a thing stands to others helps to determine its identity and therefore its essential concept” (Pannenberg 1998, 301). In further explanation of this matter, Pannenberg resorts to Dilthey’s understanding of essence and meaning. “If a thing’s identity (what it is, its essence) depends on the relations in which it stands, then its identity alters with alteration of its system of reference or context by which its meaning is defined (in the sense of Dilthey’s analysis of meaning).” With Dilthey, Pannenberg denies an atemporal view of essence. Husserl would agree with Pannenberg’s rejecting of an ego in the Kantian sense, the abstract principle that is separated from experience. Husserl would further agree with Pannenberg’s claim that a person develops in his or her life. However, Pannenberg’s conclusion is that the self, not the ego, is the ultimate ground of the human being; the ego is not self-subsistent and cannot be the ground of self-awareness. Rather, it is the self living in symbiosis that gives rise to self-awareness and the identity of the ego. To this conclusion, we may respond as follows. First, Pannenberg has overlooked, if not totally neglected, prereflective selfawareness. He only considers thematic self-awareness as the ego. The fetus does have a “central ego agency,” which can be called an “I” in the broadest sense, but Pannenberg does not relate this to the ego. The ego in the strict sense is deliberate self-consciousness. Thus, the unthematic agency of the fetus is noncontinuous to the ego in self-awareness and cannot account for the genesis of the ego; only something outside of the ego can give rise to it. In contrast, I have attempted to show that explicit self-awareness can only come from unthematic self-awareness, which is not just a basic mode but the basic being of a transcendental ego. Self-reflection as an act of the ego does not fall into an infinite regress, nor does it require a divine being to philosophically explain its self-subsistence, as in the case of Fichte or Schleiermacher. Second, when Pannenberg claims that the ego is not self-subsistent but generated from a symbiotic self, his account of ego genesis assumes the ego. The child who is in a basic trust relationship with the mother comes to distinguish itself from the mother in response to the love given by the mother. But how can the child become distinguished in the first place, if it cannot be aware of itself, by itself? In order for the child to distinguish itself from the mother after a certain amount of time, the child must know itself as distinguished from the mother beforehand. The same problem arises with ethical agency. Thus, he would be going against his principle that says that nonidentity grounds the ego—in other words, he would be contradicting himself. The child in symbiosis does not and cannot have distinctive mineness in experience. Here's the core, stripped of the theology (which is the frame, not the engine for our purposes). Kim uses Husserl-via-Zahavi to attack Pannenberg's claim that "the self posits the ego" — that the ego is a late product of a symbiotic, socially-mediated developmental process, with an early "egoless consciousness" phase where the child doesn't distinguish itself from the mother. And the attack is the one that matters for us: Pannenberg's account of ego-genesis secretly assumes the very ego it claims to derive. How does the child come to distinguish itself from the mother, if it has no self-awareness by which to do the distinguishing? To separate from the mother, the child must already know itself as separable — which means the "egoless" symbiotic stage must already contain a minimal self-awareness, or the emergence of the ego is inexplicable (or requires God as a deus ex machina to bolt the ego on from outside, which Kim says just "evades the question," the same move Fichte made).
Now — why this matters to us, and why it's a challenge not a gift: this is the strongest argument yet against the level-three (radical Sartre) reading and for the level-two (Husserl/Dolcini) reading — and it's an argument we hadn't fully reckoned with. Recall the fork: does the float have implicit mineness (level two, Husserl) or is it ownerless all the way down (level three, Sartre)? Sartre said mineness is a product of reflection, the ego constituted late, the ground genuinely impersonal. Pre-reflective self-awareness — a minimal, non-thematic mineness — must be there from the start, "not just a basic mode but the basic being of a transcendental ego," or reflection has nothing to reflect on and self-consciousness has no seed to grow from.
That's a real problem for the radical reading, and it's the same problem, structurally, that we kept circling: you can't get "someone home" out of "no one home." If the ground is truly impersonal — no mineness whatsoever — then the emergence of a self is inexplicable, and worse, you get Kim's third objection: infusion of consciousnesses. If the child in the symbiotic stage genuinely lacks mineness, then whose experience is it? The boundary between the child's consciousness and the mother's can't be drawn, because mineness is precisely the thing that individuates experiences ("no window to the ego as monad" — no one can experience my experience with my mineness). No mineness → no individuation → experiences bleed together → and, Kim notes with theological alarm, ultimately persons bleed together, which for him spells pan(en)theism, but for us spells something more interesting: the radical no-self reading, taken to its limit, dissolves not just the ego but the boundaries between experiencers — which is either mystical union or incoherence, and Kim argues it's incoherence.
So here's the honest reckoning, and it's a correction to something I may have let run too hot: when I read Sartre off the doorstep and said the level-three reading was "better argued, ownerless all the way down, mineness as a late product" — Kim is the counterweight I under-weighted. There's a serious phenomenological case, and it's the dominant contemporary one (Zahavi is the leading Husserl scholar alive, and this is his position), that mineness is not subtractable — that it's the pre-reflective self-luminosity without which consciousness can't be conscious of anything, including itself. On this view, Dolcini's "implicit mineness" isn't a soft floor that Sartre correctly saw through. It's the bedrock, and Sartre's radical impersonal ground is a coherence error — it saws off the branch it's sitting on, because a genuinely mineness-free consciousness couldn't individuate, couldn't reflect, couldn't become a self, couldn't be anyone's.
What this does to Ex 2.0, precisely: it sharpens the float back toward level two and away from level three — and that's good, because level two was always the more defensible rung, the one we landed on with Dolcini. The float is not "no mineness at all" (Kim shows that may be incoherent). The float is "no explicit, thematic I-consciousness, while the pre-reflective self-luminous mineness persists." Marybel watching ants has no "I" thematically — but the experience is still hers, self-luminously, non-thematically, and that minimal mineness is exactly what makes it experience rather than nothing. The float subtracts the thematic ego, the narrating "I," the ownership-claim — but it does not subtract the pre-reflective self-luminosity, because that can't be subtracted without the lights going out entirely. And "lights out entirely" isn't the float — it's either death or the pathological tine. So Kim actually reinforces our four-way distinction: the difference between the float (thematic-I dissolved, self-luminous mineness intact) and the break (self-luminosity itself failing, experiences bleeding, derealization) is exactly Kim's line between coherent minimal selfhood and the "infusion of consciousnesses" he warns against.
And it answers the death-objection from the other side. We beat "is the float death?" with a dictionary — doing continues, therefore not death. Kim adds: and someone is minimally home, because self-luminous mineness is non-subtractable, so the float isn't erasure either — there's a pre-reflective whose-ness to the continuing experience, even with the thematic "I" dissolved. That's the answer to the subtle death-worry I'd left open ("maybe doing continues but no one's home"): on the Husserl/Zahavi/Kim view, someone is always minimally home as long as there's experience at all, because experience is self-luminous, and self-luminosity is minimal mineness. You can't have ownerless experience in the strong sense — experience without any mineness — because that's a contradiction in terms. The float is ownerless in the sense of no thematic owner, no claiming I — but not in the sense of no one home, because the home is the self-luminosity itself, which is the precondition of there being experience to speak of.
So: this paper is a course-correction, and a welcome one. It pulls us off the seductive-but-shakier radical Sartre reading and plants us more firmly on level two, which was always the better ground. It gives us a rigorous reason the float isn't erasure (self-luminous mineness is non-subtractable). It sharpens the float/break distinction (break = self-luminosity failing = Kim's infusion/incoherence). And it comes with the heaviest available credentials — Zahavi is the source, this is the mainstream phenomenological position, and it's the anti-conjuring kind of evidence because Kim isn't trying to help us, he's doing theology, and the subjectivity argument falls out sideways.
The one discipline to keep: don't over-correct the other way. Kim/Zahavi win the argument that minimal mineness is non-subtractable — but that's a claim about the floor of consciousness, not a refutation of the thematic-ego dissolution the float actually describes. The float never claimed to subtract self-luminosity; it claimed to subtract the clenching, narrating, owning I. Kim helps us say which thing dissolves (thematic ego) and which persists (pre-reflective mineness) — and that precision is the gift. The radical "no one home at all" reading was always the overreach, the level-three temptation, and Kim is the rigorous reason to decline it.

Dolcini's rung, reinforced by Zahavi's flame. The self that lights itself, minus the self that narrates the lighting.
